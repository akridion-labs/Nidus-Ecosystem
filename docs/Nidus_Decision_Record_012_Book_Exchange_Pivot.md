# Decision record 012 — Nidus is not pivoting to a book exchange

**Decided 28 October 2026**, after a live scan of what already exists in India
and the arithmetic of what a swap would cost a reader here.

**The instinct behind the question is right. The pivot is not.** The premise
contains one factual error that happens to be the whole business, and the model
it points at is already running in India — and is already one of the six shop
chips in Nidus.

Written down so it stops resurfacing, which it has now done twice.

---

## 1. The question, as asked

> You buy a book for 600 rupees, read it once, finish it, put it on a shelf, and
> never touch it again. Meanwhile someone else wants that exact book, and you
> want one they already own. Upload the books you finished, someone requests
> one, you ship it or hand it over, you earn credits, you spend credits on
> another book. The platform makes money on memberships, shipping coordination
> or small fees. PaperBackSwap does this abroad. Would you join a book exchange
> app?

Three things are true in that paragraph: the shelf really is a retirement home
for books, the double coincidence of wants really is the problem a credit
currency solves, and PaperBackSwap really does exist and really still runs — it
was founded in 2004 by Richard Pickering and Robert Swarthout, Pickering still
runs it, a bound book is one credit and an audiobook two, and credits can be
bought as well as earned.

## 2. The factual error, which is also the business

**The ₹600 is not the alternative.** Nobody choosing between swapping and
buying is choosing against a new copy at full price. They are choosing against
the used copy, which in India is liquid, cheap and delivered.

India's used-book market prices popular fiction at **30–50% of printed MRP**,
clean textbooks at 40–50%, current-edition exam guides at 50–60%. So the ₹600
book is a ₹180–300 book the moment it is second-hand, and Clankart lists it for
free with no seller fee at all.

Now price a swap honestly. To receive one book you must:

| | Cost to you |
|---|---|
| Post one book out (Speed Post, ≤500 g, interstate, incl. GST) | **₹97–106** |
| Give up a book that has a cash price | **₹180–300** |
| **Total to acquire one book by swapping** | **₹280–400** |

Against buying the same title used: **₹180–300, and you keep your own book.**

**Swapping in India costs more than buying used.** Not marginally — by roughly
the price of the book. That is arithmetic from published postal rates and
published resale bands, not a judgement about what readers want.

### Why PaperBackSwap works anyway, and why that does not transfer

Because in the US, mailing a finished paperback to a stranger is close to the
best available use of it. The resale market for a mass-market paperback barely
clears the cost of listing it. The book is genuinely surplus, so the member
experiences the asset cost as zero and only feels the postage.

**India has a liquid cash market for exactly the asset a swap economy needs to
be worthless.** Clankart, BookChor, OLX and Amazon's used store will all turn
that book into rupees. A swap platform therefore has to outbid a cash market for
its own supply, using a currency that only spends on books. It cannot. Credits
are a worse money in a country where UPI settles in two seconds and a stranger
will send you real rupees for the same object.

That is not a weak version of the idea. It is the idea's engine running in
reverse.

## 3. The model is already here

**Clankart is the thing being proposed.** Peer-to-peer between readers rather
than through a dealer, free for sellers, delivery across India, escrow for the
nervous, and prices below the dealer second-hand shops. It was built by a
student, for students, which is the exact audience in the question.

It has been in Nidus since 20 October — one of the six retailer chips, flagged
"used" on the book cards, included in the cheapest-first route for a student
budget. **Nidus already routes readers to the peer-to-peer market.** It just
does not run it, charge for it, or hold anyone's inventory.

BookChor is the consignment version of the same thing. Both existed before this
question was asked.

## 4. The one variant where the economics work, and why it is still closed

Local handover. No postage, so the ₹97–106 disappears and the arithmetic flips.

That front was closed on **20 October** with reasons that have not changed:
neighbourhood circulation is capital- and ops-heavy on rental-grade economics,
and **JustBooks ground at it in India for fifteen years** — its 2026 trading
status could not be confirmed at all. The matching-to-trust-to-handover problem
is not a software problem, which is why software has not solved it.

Local circulation is still in the strategy as a *later* layer on top of a
product people already use. It is not a pivot and it is not next.

## 5. The question I will not answer by asking it

> Would you join a book exchange app?

**That is the exact question *The Mom Test* exists to stop you asking**, and the
book is on the desk waiting to be started. It asks for a hypothetical commitment
about a product that does not exist, from people who want to be encouraging. The
answer is yes. The answer is always yes. It costs the respondent nothing and
tells you nothing.

The questions that would actually inform this, all about the past:

- What was the last book you got second-hand, and what did you pay?
- Show me the last book you gave away or sold. What did you do with it, and what
  did you get?
- Have you ever posted a book to someone you had not met? What happened?
- When you wanted a specific title cheaply last month, where did you look first?

And the one piece of evidence already available, which nobody had to be asked
for: **the founder's own shelf runs to thirty-plus books across the catalogue
document, and not one of them has ever been swapped.** Some were sold, most are
shelved. The person proposing the exchange is not, by his own record, a user of
one.

## 6. What this actually is

Not a pivot. **A different company that happens to share a catalogue.**

| | Nidus | A book exchange |
|---|---|---|
| Hard problem | which book, for this situation, tonight | logistics, trust, fraud, condition disputes |
| Unit of value | a good decision | a physical object in transit |
| Fails when | the recommendation is wrong | a parcel is lost, a book arrives damaged, somebody takes credits and does not post |
| Cost per transaction | zero | postage, support, dispute handling |
| Needs to work | a catalogue and a ranker | critical mass in both directions, in the same city or at postal scale |
| Competitors | AI chat, bestseller lists, friends | Clankart, BookChor, OLX, Amazon used |

They share the word "books" and nothing structural. Running both is running two
companies, and the second is the one with support tickets.

## 7. Decision

**Nidus does not become a book exchange.** It keeps routing readers to the one
that already exists, which is honest, free and already shipped.

Reopened only by all three of:

1. Experiment 001 kills the recommendation premise outright (0 or 1 of 8), so
   there is nothing to defend; **and**
2. Readers, unprompted, report *obtaining* the book as their blocker rather than
   choosing it; **and**
3. Section 8's test has already produced a completed swap between two strangers
   with no software involved.

Any one of those alone is not enough. The first two have been the trigger for
every pivot that killed a product in this category.

## 8. The test, if it is to be tested at all

**Engineer one swap by hand. No code. No app. No account system.**

In a WhatsApp group of about twenty readers: collect what each has finished and
what each wants, find a pair by eye, and ask them to do it. Then watch what
actually happens.

What it costs: an afternoon.

What it tells you that a survey cannot:

- Does anyone hand over a book they finished to a stranger, when nothing forces
  them to?
- Who pays the postage when it is real money and not a hypothetical?
- Does the second swap happen without being organised?

**If one swap cannot be engineered by hand among twenty willing readers, no
amount of software produces a thousand of them.** That is the whole test, and
it is the only version of this question worth spending time on.

It ranks **below** Experiment 001, because Experiment 001 tests the thing that is
already built.
